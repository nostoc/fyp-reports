\section{Evaluation Aims and Questions}
\label{sec:evaluation-aims}

The evaluation has been guided by the following questions. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Do the four smart contracts enforce their intended functions and access rules correctly?
    \item Are duplicate actions, replay attempts, invalid signatures, and emergency-report abuse rejected as designed?
    \item Are the relayer, queue, AI services, IPFS service, and blockchain correctly integrated into one working pipeline?
    \item What response time, throughput, block interval, and gas cost does the private network offer for a civic-reporting workload?
\end{enumerate}

The remaining sections of this chapter answer these questions in turn.

\section{Smart-Contract Functional Testing}
\label{sec:contract-testing}

\subsection{Method}

Contract behaviour has been tested with Hardhat and Chai on a local, fast in-process blockchain, which lets every test run quickly while still executing the real compiled contract bytecode. Each test deploys a fresh contract instance, so that no test can be influenced by state left behind by an earlier one. Hardhat's time-manipulation helpers has been used to advance the simulated chain clock directly, where a test needs a voting window or a penalty period to expire, rather than waiting in real time. Therefore, a six-hour voting window or a 30-day penalty can be exercised in milliseconds.

\subsection{Functional Test Procedure}

For Reporting.sol, both valid and deliberately invalid reports are submitted, exercising every custom revert condition alongside the success path. Validation, verification, and rejection-review votes are cast from multiple accounts, voting deadlines are advanced, and the resulting status transition has been checked against the state machine defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}. Pagination, per-citizen lookup, and the authority action history has been exercised so that the read-side of the contract is verified as same as the write-side.

For EmergencyReporting.sol, the fast-track path has been checked end to end. A submission opens immediately without a validation phase, an authority can resolve it directly from Open or after claiming it with startWork, and a reclassified report correctly locks its citizen's pseudonym for the configured penalty window. For OpinionPolling.sol, poll creation restrictions, nullifier-based one-vote enforcement, and deadline handling has been covered. For AuthorityMultiSig.sol, proposal creation, automatic self-approval, and quorum-based execution ahas been covered for both Super Admin and authority-worker changes.

\section{Security and Sybil-Resistance Testing}
\label{sec:security-testing}

\subsection{Method}

Security.ts is dedicated to the specific risks a public-facing civic-reporting system has to withstand. A citizen or bot retrying the same action, a citizen attempting to use more than one identity to influence a vote, and an authority action being triggered by an unauthorized account are some examples for such risks. Table~\ref{tab:security-cases} summarizes the six checks in this suite, each targeting a distinct attack surface identified during the architecture design in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Security and Sybil-resistance test cases}
\label{tab:security-cases}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.10\textwidth}|p{0.80\textwidth}}
    
    Case & What is checked \\
    \hline
    T6.1 & A used submission nullifier is replayed 100 times in a row; every attempt is expected to revert \\
    \hline
    T6.2 & A non-relayer account cannot call submitReport directly \\
    \hline
    T6.3 & A second validation vote from the same citizen pseudonym is rejected even when a fresh vote nullifier is used \\
    \hline
    T6.4 & A second poll vote with the same nullifier is rejected \\
    \hline
    T6.5 & A reclassified emergency report locks its citizen's pseudonym for 30 days, and submission succeeds again once the penalty period has elapsed \\
    \hline
    T6.6 & A second verification vote from the same citizen pseudonym is rejected during the post-resolution phase \\
    
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Running the replay case 100 times, rather than once, is a deliberate choice. A single successful rejection could be a coincidence of test ordering, whereas 100 consecutive rejections give confidence that the nullifier check is a behaving as designed. The other five cases are each run once, because they are deterministic and do not depend on a sequence of events. More details on the test cases are provided in Appendix~\ref{apndx:security-test-cases}.

\section{Blockchain Performance Benchmark}
\label{sec:performance-benchmark} 

\subsection{Method}

A dedicated Hardhat script measures the private network's practical performance directly against the live running deployed blockchain, rather than relying on theoretical figures for Clique PoA. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-measurements} shows the measurements that has been measured.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth, height=0.6\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 5/benchmark-measurements.png}
    \caption{Stages of the blockchain performance benchmark}
    \label{fig:benchmark-measurements}
\end{figure}

Gas for submitReport() was averaged over five submissions run before any other benchmark load, so the figure reflects a quiet network. End-to-end latency was timed from dispatch until the receipt confirmed inclusion in a block, matching the wait a citizen sees after the relayer submits on their behalf. Throughput was estimated by broadcasting a batch of transactions in parallel with explicit nonce and fee assignment, then dividing the number that confirmed by the elapsed time.

The Clique block interval was derived from consecutive sealed block timestamps rather than the configured period alone, because live validator timing can differ slightly from the nominal setting. Vote transactions were costed with the same five-sample method used for report submission. Batch-finalization gas was captured by pointing at a short voting-window contract, opening several reports against it, and invoking the finalize routine once to close them in a single transaction.

\section{IPFS and Identity Simulator Checks}
\label{sec:ipfs-identity-testing}

\subsection{Method}

A manual integration script uploads files of increasing size through the IPFS HTTP service, retrieves each one by its returned CID, and compares the retrieved content hash against the original. This directly exercises the content-addressing guarantee the architecture relies on. If a CID resolves to content whose hash matches what was uploaded, the integrity property is behaving as designed. Concurrent uploads and an unreachable-service case are also checked, so that both normal and failure conditions are covered.

\section{\ac{E2E} (E2E) Manual Testing }
\label{sec:e2e-testing}

A complete lifecycle scenario has been executed across every layer of the system. A simulated citizen registers via government identity simulator, and receives a batch of signed, single-use tickets. Then submit a standard report, which moves through AI moderation, IPFS storage, and finally on-chain submission. Three other citizens cast validation upvotes during voting window, and report moves to state Open after finalizaton. An authority claims the report, start working on it, and posts an update, updaing report state to InProgress. Then upload evidence to support resolution of the issue, and mark the report as solved, moving report state to PendingVerification. Two citizens accept the resolution by submitting positive verification votes, moving report state to Closed. 

In addition to this happy path, full manual E2E UI test cases and together with screenshots are included in Section~\ref{sec:manual-e2e}. 


\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:testing-summary}

This chapter explained how the prototype was exercised and what the runs produced. In total, 128 automated cases passed. 75 smart-contract tests across five files, 25 relayer unit tests across three specs, 20 AI moderation checks across two files, and 8 \ac{IPFS} integration checks. Six manual \ac{E2E} walkthroughs and a benchmark against the live private network complemented that automated coverage. On the benchmark host, submitReport() averaged 309{,}056 gas, submit-to-confirm latency averaged 5{,}017\,ms, a batch of twenty concurrent transactions reached 0.87 TPS, and the observed Clique block interval averaged 5.0\,s. Appendix~\ref{app:test-cases} lists every case for traceability. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} picks up from those figures and compares the measured behaviour with the targets set in the design chapters.
