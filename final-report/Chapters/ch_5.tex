\chapter{Testing and Performance Evaluation}
\label{ch:testing}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:testing-introduction}

This chapter presents how the prototype described in Chapters~\ref{ch:architecture} and~\ref{ch:implementation} has been verified. Automated tests have been written alongside the implementation for every major component: the four smart contracts, the Sybil-resistance controls built into them, the backend relayer's cryptographic and validation logic, the AI moderation aggregator, and the IPFS storage service. A dedicated benchmark script has also been written to measure blockchain throughput, latency, and gas cost directly against the deployed private network.

Figure~\ref{fig:evaluation-structure} shows how the evaluation is organized. Functional correctness, Sybil-resistance and security, service integration, and blockchain performance are each covered by a dedicated test suite, and every suite is placed alongside the code it tests so that it runs as part of normal development rather than as a separate, disconnected activity.

\section{Evaluation Aims and Questions}
\label{sec:evaluation-aims}

The evaluation has been guided by the following questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Do the four smart contracts enforce their intended functions and access rules correctly?
    \item Are duplicate actions, replay attempts, invalid signatures, and emergency-report abuse rejected as designed?
    \item Are the relayer, queue, AI services, IPFS service, and blockchain correctly integrated into one working pipeline?
    \item What response time, throughput, block interval, and gas cost does the private network offer for a civic-reporting workload?
\end{enumerate}

The remaining sections of this chapter answer these questions in turn.

\section{Smart-Contract Functional Testing}
\label{sec:contract-testing}

\subsection{Method}

Contract behaviour is tested with Hardhat and Chai on a local, fast in-process blockchain, which lets every test run quickly while still executing the real compiled contract bytecode. Each test deploys a fresh contract instance, so that no test can be influenced by state left behind by an earlier one. Where a test needs a voting window or a penalty period to expire, Hardhat's time-manipulation helpers are used to advance the simulated chain clock directly, rather than waiting in real time, so that a six-hour voting window or a 30-day penalty can be exercised in milliseconds without weakening what is actually being checked.

\subsection{Functional Test Procedure}

For \texttt{Reporting.sol}, both valid and deliberately invalid reports are submitted, exercising every custom revert condition alongside the success path. Validation, verification, and rejection-review votes are cast from multiple accounts, voting deadlines are advanced, and the resulting status transition is checked against the state machine defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}. Pagination, per-citizen lookup, and the authority action history are also exercised so that the read-side of the contract is verified with the same care as the write-side.

For \texttt{EmergencyReporting.sol}, the fast-track path is checked end to end: a submission opens immediately without a validation phase, an authority can resolve it directly from \texttt{Open} or after claiming it with \texttt{startWork}, and a reclassified report correctly locks its citizen's pseudonym for the configured penalty window. For \texttt{OpinionPolling.sol}, poll creation restrictions, nullifier-based one-vote enforcement, and deadline handling are covered. For \texttt{AuthorityMultiSig.sol}, proposal creation, automatic self-approval, and quorum-based execution are covered for both Super Admin and authority-worker changes.

\section{Security and Sybil-Resistance Testing}
\label{sec:security-testing}

\subsection{Method}

\texttt{Security.ts} is dedicated to the specific risks a public-facing civic-reporting system has to withstand: a citizen or bot retrying the same action, a citizen attempting to use more than one identity to influence a vote, and an authority action being triggered by an account that was never granted the role. Table~\ref{tab:security-cases} summarizes the six checks in this suite, each targeting a distinct attack surface identified during the architecture design in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Security and Sybil-resistance test cases}
\label{tab:security-cases}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.16\textwidth}|p{0.84\textwidth}}
    
    Case & What is checked \\
    \hline
    T6.1 & A used submission nullifier is replayed 100 times in a row; every attempt is expected to revert \\
    \hline
    T6.2 & A non-relayer account cannot call \texttt{submitReport} directly \\
    \hline
    T6.3 & A second validation vote from the same citizen pseudonym is rejected even when a fresh vote nullifier is used \\
    \hline
    T6.4 & A second poll vote with the same nullifier is rejected \\
    \hline
    T6.5 & A reclassified emergency report locks its citizen's pseudonym for 30 days, and submission succeeds again once the penalty period has elapsed \\
    \hline
    T6.6 & A second verification vote from the same citizen pseudonym is rejected during the post-resolution phase \\
    
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Running the replay case 100 times, rather than once, is a deliberate choice: a single successful rejection could be a coincidence of test ordering, whereas 100 consecutive rejections give confidence that the nullifier check is a genuine invariant of the contract rather than an artefact of one particular test run.

\section{Relayer Unit Testing}
\label{sec:relayer-testing}

\subsection{Method}

The relayer's cryptographic and validation logic is tested in isolation with Jest, co-located with the source files it exercises, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:relayer-test-suites}. Testing this logic directly, rather than only through HTTP requests, means each check runs in milliseconds and pinpoints the exact function responsible if a regression is introduced.

\texttt{reporting.service.spec.ts} covers government ticket verification (T2.1), citizen payload signature verification (T2.2), citizen pseudonym derivation (T2.3), vote payload verification (T2.4), and environment configuration validation (T2.5). Real Ethers.js wallets are used to sign and verify messages in these tests, so the actual cryptographic recovery logic is exercised rather than a mocked stand-in. \texttt{ai-oracle.service.spec.ts} separately covers the canonical JSON hashing and signing logic the relayer uses when it talks to the AI aggregator, and \texttt{app.controller.spec.ts} checks that the NestJS application bootstraps correctly.

\section{AI Moderation Testing}
\label{sec:ai-testing}

\subsection{Method}

The AI oracle test suite, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ai-test-categories}, is split into two files that mirror the two concerns of the moderation service: whether a request is allowed to reach the aggregator at all, and whether the aggregator reaches the correct decision once it does.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth,height=0.4\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 5/ai-test-categories.png}
    \caption{Categories covered by the AI oracle test suite}
    \label{fig:ai-test-categories}
\end{figure}

\texttt{test\_api\_security.py} sends requests directly to the running aggregator to check the request-protection layer described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}: a fully valid request, a missing or wrong API key, a forged relayer signature, a replayed nonce, an oversized file, and a disallowed file count or MIME type. \texttt{test\_classification.py} then checks the semantic decisions made once a request passes that layer, sending realistic civic reports, hate speech, commercial spam, off-topic text, and an emergency civic report, together with safe, corrupted, and unsafe images, and confirming that every response includes a usable decision and explanation.

\section{Blockchain Performance Benchmark}
\label{sec:performance-benchmark}

\subsection{Method}

A dedicated Hardhat script, \texttt{smart-contracts/scripts/benchmark.ts}, measures the private network's practical performance directly against a running deployment, rather than relying on theoretical figures for Clique PoA. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-measurements} shows the six measurements the script takes in sequence.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 5/benchmark-measurements.png}
    \caption{Stages of the blockchain performance benchmark}
    \label{fig:benchmark-measurements}
\end{figure}

Gas cost for \texttt{submitReport()} is measured first, over five submissions, so that a representative average is available before any other activity affects network conditions. End-to-end latency is then measured from the moment a transaction is dispatched to the moment its receipt confirms inclusion in a block, giving a citizen-facing figure rather than a purely theoretical one. Throughput is measured by dispatching a batch of transactions concurrently, with explicit nonce and fee management so that the batch is genuinely submitted together, and dividing the confirmed count by the elapsed time. The Clique block interval is read directly from consecutive block timestamps rather than assumed from the configured period, which lets the recorded interval reflect real validator behaviour. Voting gas is measured the same way as submission gas, and batch-finalization gas is measured by submitting reports to a short-window contract instance and finalizing them together in one call.

\section{IPFS and Identity Simulator Checks}
\label{sec:ipfs-identity-testing}

\subsection{Method}

A manual integration script uploads files of increasing size through the IPFS HTTP service, retrieves each one by its returned CID, and compares the retrieved content hash against the original. This directly exercises the content-addressing guarantee the architecture relies on: if a CID resolves to content whose hash matches what was uploaded, the integrity property that the design depends on is demonstrated in practice rather than assumed from IPFS documentation alone. Concurrent uploads and an unreachable-service case are also checked, so that both normal and failure conditions are covered.

\section{End-to-End Workflow Scenario}
\label{sec:e2e-testing}

A complete lifecycle scenario has been exercised across every layer of the system at once: a simulated citizen authenticates and receives a signed ticket, submits a standard report, and observes the ordered progress events described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} as the report moves through AI moderation, IPFS storage, and on-chain submission. Three further simulated citizens then cast validation upvotes, the voting window is finalized, and the report moves to \texttt{Open}. An authority claims the report, posts an update, and marks it solved, moving it to \texttt{PendingVerification}. Two simulated citizens accept the resolution, and the report reaches \texttt{Closed}, completing the full report lifecycle shown in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}.
