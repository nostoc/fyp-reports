\section{Overview}
\label{sec:results-overview}

Chapter~\ref{ch:testing} recorded 128 passing test cases across four suites, a dedicated blockchain performance benchmark, and a complete \ac{E2E} lifecycle scenario. This chapter interprets that evidence against the objectives set out in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} and the architecture decisions justified in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}. Taken together, the results show that the prototype correctly enforces the intended reporting, validation, and governance rules on-chain, integrates its off-chain services into one working pipeline, and does so at response times and costs that are well suited to a civic-reporting workload. The chapter also places the design in context by comparing it with the citizen-reporting and blockchain-governance frameworks introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, and closes by outlining how the validated prototype can be extended further.

\section{Functional Results}
\label{sec:functional-results}

\subsection{Smart-Contract Enforced Workflows}

All 75 smart-contract cases pass in a single Hardhat run, covering    {Reporting.sol} (41 cases),    {EmergencyReporting.sol} (13 cases),    {OpinionPolling.sol} (10 cases),    {AuthorityMultiSig.sol} (4 cases), and the cross-contract    {Security.ts} suite (7 cases). Every state transition described in the report lifecycle in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}, submission, community validation, authority action, post-resolution verification, and rejection review, is exercised directly against the compiled contract bytecode, together with pagination, per-citizen lookup, and the authority action history. This result demonstrates that the reporting rules are not merely suggestions enforced by the web interface. They are guaranteed by the blockchain itself, so an invalid state change is rejected regardless of which client submits it. The emergency and opinion-polling contracts add a fast-track path and a separate consultation mechanism, and both are covered end to end, confirming that the four contracts designed in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture} are functionally complete and behave as intended.

\subsection{Integrated Relayer, AI Moderation, and Storage Pipeline}

Twenty-five relayer unit tests exercise the checks that run before anything is queued. Government-ticket recovery, citizen signature verification, pseudonym derivation, and vote-payload validation, all against real Ethers.js wallets rather than stubs. Twenty AI oracle tests cover both sides of the moderation path, request signing, nonce replay limits, and file caps on one hand, and civic, hate, spam, off-topic, and emergency text together with safe, corrupted, and unsafe images on the other. Every outcome returns an explanation string. Eight \ac{IPFS} integration tests round out the set by fetching uploaded content through its returned \ac{CID} and confirming the hash, for payloads from roughly 1\,KB to 5\,MB and under concurrent uploads.

Together these suites cover every hop between a citizen submission and the on-chain record. Their combined pass rate supports the ordering chosen in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}. Verify at the relayer, moderate with AI, store off-chain, then anchor on the private ledger.

\subsection{\ac{E2E} Lifecycle and Duplicate Suppression Confirmation}

Six manual \ac{E2E} scenarios were run across the full stack, separate from the automated suites. The standard-report walkthrough took a citizen submission through AI moderation, \ac{IPFS} storage, on-chain validation, authority resolution, and community verification until the case reached \texttt{Closed}. A client-side duplicate check also flagged an active report in the same category within a 1\,km radius, blocking a second submission before it reached the relayer and saving unnecessary gas.

Those runs show that the pieces tested in isolation still line up when wired together. Together with the 128 passing unit and integration cases, they give a practical picture of a working, deduplicated reporting flow rather than a collection of disconnected services.

\section{Security and Privacy Outcomes}
\label{sec:security-privacy-findings}

The Security.ts suite focuses on misuse patterns that matter for a system open to the public, and every case in it passes. Replaying a spent submission nullifier one hundred times in a row produced one hundred rejections, which shows the one-ticket-one-action rule is enforced by the contract rather than by client-side habit. The same pseudonym cannot cast a second validation vote, verification vote, or poll vote even when a new vote nullifier is supplied. A false-emergency reclassification also freezes that pseudonym on the emergency contract for thirty days and then lifts the block automatically. Forged government tickets and altered report payloads are turned away at the relayer before they reach the chain. That split matches the design in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}. Signatures checked off-chain, nullifiers checked on-chain.

Privacy rests on the pairing of pseudonyms with single-use tickets. When a citizen reports, votes, or answers a poll, the ledger stores no government ID, name, or other direct identifier. Contract state holds a pseudonym derived from the wallet, ticket references, and signature material. Someone reading the chain later can see that an action occurred and whether it followed the rules, but not which institutional identity performed it. For local issues where retaliation is a real concern, that separation is enough to make participation usable, and the passing security cases are what confirm those identifying fields never get written.

The measurements in Table~\ref{tab:performance-snapshot} line up with the choices made in Chapters~\ref{ch:architecture} and~\ref{ch:implementation}. Citizens were never meant to wait on a mined block before moving on. The relayer pays gas, accepts the payload, and returns while BullMQ finishes moderation, \ac{IPFS} upload, and anchoring behind the scenes with \ac{SSE} progress events.

On the network itself, the average Clique interval sat at five seconds and submit-to-confirm latency stayed under six, which is short enough that those background steps feel responsive once they start. Gas was also lower for the actions that repeat as a case moves forward. castValidationVote() and batched window finalization cost far less than an initial submitReport(), so community checkpoints and cron-driven closes do not dominate operating cost as volume grows.

This achievement is a direct consequence of combining several separate research directions, privacy-preserving identity, AI-assisted moderation, community validation, and contract-based governance, into a single tested prototype. The centralized platforms remain valuable references for interface simplicity and proven civic engagement patterns, and the recorded results in this project show that a blockchain-based design can retain those qualities. Citizens still submit a report through an ordinary web form, still see prompt confirmation, and are never asked to hold cryptocurrency or manage a wallet directly, since the relayer absorbs that complexity as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. Compared with the blockchain-based complaint systems, the proposed framework additionally decouples citizen identity from on-chain records and applies AI moderation before content is anchored. Compared with PriRPT's stronger cryptographic privacy guarantee, it trades some of that cryptographic strength for a lighter-weight ticket-based mechanism that is fully implemented, integrated with the rest of the pipeline, and directly validated by the passing Security suite in Chapter~\ref{ch:testing}.

\section{Key Strengths of the Design}
\label{sec:design-benefits}

The recorded results support the following strengths of the implemented design.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Reporting, validation, resolution, and governance rules are enforced by contract code, so they hold regardless of which client interacts with the system.
    \item Report state and authority actions are kept in an auditable, tamper-evident ledger, while large media files are kept off-chain and linked by verifiable content hashes.
    \item Citizens interact through a normal web \ac{UI}, and the relayer signs and pays for chain transactions so they do not need their own ETH or MetaMask for everyday reporting.
    \item BullMQ runs moderation, \ac{IPFS} upload, and anchoring as separate steps, and \ac{SSE} streams status back to the browser instead of leaving the user on a blank wait with no detail.
    \item Submission, validation, and poll nullifiers are checked on-chain, and pseudonyms hide wallet addresses while still letting the contracts enforce one action per citizen per phase, as the Security suite demonstrated.
    \item Classifiers run before anything is written to an immutable ledger, which avoids the problem noted in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} of harmful text or images becoming impossible to remove once stored on-chain.
    \item Emergency reporting and multisig authority management sit in their own contracts, so urgent cases and worker provisioning are not bolted onto the standard report lifecycle as afterthoughts.
\end{itemize}

The points above do not rest on any one technique in isolation. They come from wiring together a permissioned \ac{PoA} network, relayer-paid submissions, off-chain \ac{IPFS} storage, ticket-based pseudonyms, and pre-chain moderation, then showing that the full path works in the tests recorded in Chapter~\ref{ch:testing}.

\section{Scope for Future Enhancement}
\label{sec:results-scope}

The results in this chapter validate the core architecture and give a solid, tested foundation to build on. Several enhancements are natural next steps for extending that foundation toward broader deployment. Replacing the government identity simulator with a full zero-knowledge credential protocol would strengthen the privacy guarantee beyond ticket-based pseudonymity. Expanding the AI moderation evaluation with a larger, multilingual civic dataset would build on the demonstrated pipeline to establish broader accuracy across Sinhala, Tamil, and English content. Evaluating a decentralized oracle configuration for the AI aggregator would extend the current centralized moderation service toward the fuller decentralization goal discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}. Finally, adding automated frontend testing, an accessibility review, and a supervised pilot deployment, would extend the current component and integration testing toward validated real-world usability. 
\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:findings-summary}

The recorded evidence supports a clear and consistent conclusion. The prototype correctly enforces its intended reporting, validation, and governance rules on a live permissioned blockchain. The system integrates relayer, AI moderation, and storage services into one working pipeline, and does so within response times and costs well suited to civic reporting. All four project objectives are backed by dedicated, independently executable test evidence. The completed \ac{E2E} lifecycle scenario confirms that these individually verified components genuinely work together. Compared with the centralized and blockchain-based frameworks reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, the implemented system combines a broader set of privacy, moderation, community-validation, and governance features into a single tested design, while retaining the citizen-facing simplicity that has made platforms like FixMyStreet and SeeClickFix successful. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} builds on these findings to summarize the project's overall contribution and outline its future direction.
