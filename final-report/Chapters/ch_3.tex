\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:architecture-introduction}

This chapter presents the design of the local governance reporting system. The system is divided into clear layers. Identity checking, content processing, storage, and public record keeping are separated, with each responsibility handled by the part of the system best suited to it. Facts that must be trusted by every participant and never quietly altered, such as a report's status, a vote count, or an authority's action, are recorded on a private blockchain. Uploading images and running \ac{AI} moderation are time consuming and data intensive tasks. Therefore, these tasks are handled off-chain. The verifiable hash of the images and also the verdict of the moderation are stored in the blockchain.

Standard civic report submissions, emergency reporting, community voting, public opinion polling, and authority works are included in the designed system. Figure~\ref{fig:component-interaction} gives a high-level view of how the main components communicate before the rest of the chapter explains each part and the reasoning behind it. Citizen participation is pseudonymous on-chain. A citizen authenticates through a government identity check and receives signed, single-use tickets, consistent with the identity-simulation scope adopted for this prototype. The sections that follow explain how these tickets support anonymous but accountable participation throughout the reporting, voting, and polling workflows.

\section{Overall Layered Architecture}
\label{sec:layered-architecture}

A hybrid on-chain and off-chain design is used because the two environments are capable of different tasks. The blockchain is well suited to recording facts that must be trusted by every participant and never silently altered, such as the status of a report or a vote tally. However, writing large media files or running machine learning inference directly on-chain would be time consuming and expensive to replicate across every node. The system offers both auditability and practical performance by keeping heavy work off-chain, while anchoring only its outcome and a verifiable hash on-chain. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item On-Chain Environment:  Executed on a private, permissioned \ac{PoA} network. It maintains minimal, immutable state variables. It includes report metadata, opinion polling metadata, lifecycle state transitions, cryptographic content hashes, single-use ticket nullifiers, \ac{RBAC} configurations, multi-signature governance proposals, and community voting tallies.
    \item Off-Chain Environment: Comprises data-intensive and computationally heavy microservices, including multimedia storage on \ac{IPFS}, automated \ac{AI} content moderation, gasless transaction orchestration via a NestJS middleware relayer, automated wallet funding, and scheduled background cron jobs for voting window finalization.
\end{enumerate}

These layer boundaries are drawn along trust and technology. The identity simulator is decoupled from the relayer because it represents a different administrative authority. AI and IPFS services are comparatively slow and are best isolated behind asynchronous jobs rather than called synchronously. Consequently, the relayer and queue layer are maintained in a separate location from AI and IPFS. The blockchain layer is kept as thin as possible because every extra byte stored or computation executed on-chain is replicated and paid for by every node in the network. Therefore, only the facts that genuinely need a tamper-evident public record are stored on the blockchain.

The blockchain uses chain ID 1337 and a 5 $s$ Clique period. Three validator nodes seal blocks, and a fourth node does not mine but instead exposes the \ac{JSON}-\ac{RPC} interface used by the rest of the system, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:validator-topology}. A 5 $s$ block period is short enough to give citizens fast confirmation feedback for a civic reporting workload, while still being long enough for three validators to agree reliably without specialised hardware. Separating the \ac{RPC}-only node from the three sealing validators means that read traffic from the \ac{DApp} and write traffic from the relayer can never compete with or accidentally disrupt the block-sealing process. If the \ac{RPC} node is restarted or briefly overloaded block production continues unaffected on the other three nodes. Direct reads are made by the \ac{DApp} through this \ac{RPC} interface. Citizen writes are normally sent through the relayer. Authority transactions are signed with MetaMask.

\section{Trust Boundaries}
\label{sec:trust-boundaries}

% The designed system contains multiple trust boundaries. Explicitly identifying these boundaries, rather than treating the system as one uniformly trusted entity clarifies which component must behave correctly for a given guarantee to hold. This distinction is essential for understanding what the  blockchain's tamper-evidence does and does not ensure. 
%
% \begin{enumerate}
%     \item Citizen device boundary: The citizen seed, derived wallet, ticket batch, and optional encrypted session are handled in the browser. A compromised browser can expose these values.
%     \item Identity simulator boundary: The simulator sees the government ID and password. It is trusted to check the simulated citizen record and to issue valid tickets only after successful authentication.
%     \item Relayer boundary: The relayer sees report text, location, images, ticket data, and the citizen wallet address. It is trusted to verify requests and to submit only approved data.
%     \item AI boundary: The aggregator and classifier services receive report content. Their decisions are advisory off-chain gates and are not blockchain consensus.
%     \item Storage boundary: The IPFS service receives the accepted complaint envelope. No authentication is implemented at the service level. Network isolation is therefore assumed.
%     \item Blockchain boundary: Validators are trusted collectively to operate the private network. Contract rules are shared by all nodes, but the permissioned validator group is not fully trustless.
% \end{enumerate}

The system does not rely on the assumption that every layer is honest. Each major component sits behind its own trust line. It defines what it can read, what it can write, and what happens if it misbehaves. That framing helps separate what the blockchain ledger can prove after the fact from what still depends on application code, operators, or user devices.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Citizen device: The browser stores the citizen seed, derived wallet, ticket batch, and any encrypted session data. A compromised or tampered client can leak all of these.
    \item Identity simulator: The prototype login sends a government ID and password to this service. It must match the simulated citizen record and release signed tickets only after authentication succeeds.
    \item Relayer: The relayer handles report text, location, images, ticket data, and the citizen wallet address before a transaction is broadcast. It is expected to validate each request and pass through only submissions that cleared the earlier checks.
    \item AI moderation: Aggregator and classifier services also process report content. Their accept or reject outcomes gate submission off-chain. Validators do not re run those models as part of consensus.
    \item Storage: The accepted complaint envelope is written to \ac{IPFS}. The storage service itself does not enforce authentication in the prototype, so who can reach it is left to network isolation and deployment policy.
    \item Blockchain: A fixed set of validators maintains the private network and runs the same contract logic on each node. Integrity of on-chain state still rests on that permissioned group rather than on an open, anonymous mining pool.
\end{enumerate}

These boundaries show that blockchain integrity does not remove trust from identity checking, moderation, storage access, or endpoint operation.

\section{Privacy and Security Design}
\label{sec:privacy-security-design}

Citizen identity is decoupled from the public report record through a government identity check. A government ID and password are verified by the identity simulator, and government-signed, single-use ticket IDs are issued only after this check succeeds. Each ticket behaves as a nullifier. The nullifier gives evidence that one unit of participation was issued to an eligible citizen, and the contract's nullifier mapping stops that same ticket from being used a second time. This ticket-based approach is used because it lets the prototype enforce single-action-per-eligible-citizen immediately using well-understood signature and mapping checks. This approach preserves the possibility of introducing a stronger anonymous-credential or \ac{ZKP}  system at a later stage, without requiring how the relayer, the contracts, or the voting logic consume tickets.

% \begin{figure}[!htb]
%     \centering
%     \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth,height=0.6\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/authentication-workflow.png}
%     \caption{Citizen authentication, ticket issuance, and pseudonym derivation}
%     \label{fig:authentication-workflow}
% \end{figure}

A citizen wallet is derived deterministically from a citizen seed in the browser. The relayer derives an on-chain pseudonym as given in Eq.(\ref{eq:pseudonym}).

\begin{equation}
    P = \operatorname{keccak256}\left(A \parallel D\right),
    \label{eq:pseudonym}
\end{equation}

where \(P\) is the citizen pseudonym, \(A\) is the citizen wallet address, \(D\) is a configured domain salt, and \(\parallel\) denotes packed concatenation. Public report entries therefore carry \(P\) instead of \(A\). The same citizen always produces the same pseudonym, so the contract can tell whether two actions came from one wallet even though the real address never appears on-chain. That property is intentional. Later sections rely on it to enforce one vote per citizen and one report per ticket without storing personal identifiers in the ledger. For this prototype, that level of indirection is enough to separate civic participation from direct wallet exposure, and the same salt-and-address recipe could later be swapped for a fuller anonymous-credential flow if needed.

Before a report enters the queue, the relayer runs the checks in Figure~\ref{fig:relayer-verification}. It validates the government signature on the ticket, compares hashes for each uploaded image, and recovers the citizen signer from the \ac{ECDSA} signature over the report payload. These steps are arranged so that lightweight checks run first. An invalid or already-used ticket is rejected immediately, avoiding the cost of hash verification and signature recovery on payloads that were never authorised in the first place.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth, keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/relayer-verification.png}
    \caption{Relayer dual-signature verification pipeline}
    \label{fig:relayer-verification}
\end{figure}

Access to the \ac{AI} aggregator requires an \ac{API} key, a relayer signature, a fresh timestamp, and a nonce that has not been seen before. If the classifier does not respond or returns an error, the submission is rejected outright. The system prefers that behaviour over letting reports through during an outage. A citizen blocked by downtime can try again once moderation is back; a report that slipped past a broken gate would be written to an immutable chain and could not be undone. Rejecting on doubt avoids the worse of those two failures.

\section{Report Data and Composite Hash}
\label{sec:report-data-hash}

The complete public report content is not stored in a smart contract due to storage and gas cost constraints. The IPFS complaint envelope contains the description, category, location, image count, image metadata, base64-encoded image data, and storage time. The blockchain record contains the IPFS CID, report hash, submission nullifier, citizen pseudonym, timestamps, status, vote counters, assigned authority, and latest authority evidence.

Each image \(I_i\) is hashed by the client and checked by the relayer as given in Eq.(\ref{eq:image-hash}).

\begin{equation}
    h_i = \operatorname{keccak256}(I_i).
    \label{eq:image-hash}
\end{equation}

The ordered image hashes are joined as \(H_I=h_1\parallel h_2\parallel\cdots\parallel h_n\). Hashing every image individually and then combining the hashes in the order the citizen uploaded them means that a single signature over the final report hash can protect the whole submission. If any image were swapped, dropped, or reordered after the citizen signed, the recomputed hash would no longer match and the relayer would reject it. The signed report hash is given by Eq.(\ref{eq:report-hash}).

\begin{equation}
    H_R = \operatorname{keccak256}\left(d \parallel t \parallel H_I\right),
    \label{eq:report-hash}
\end{equation}

where \(H_R\) is the composite report hash, \(d\) is the report description, \(t\) is the one-use ticket ID, and \(H_I\) is the ordered image-hash string. The citizen signs \(H_R\). The relayer reconstructs it and recovers the signer address.

Two separate community checkpoints are built into this lifecycle on purpose. The first, PendingValidation, protects authority time by filtering out spam, duplicates, and clearly invalid reports before any government worker is asked to act on them. The second, PendingVerification, protects citizens by making sure an authority's claim of a fix is independently confirmed by the people affected before a report is closed, rather than letting an authority close its own work unchecked. Together, the two checkpoints ensure that neither the community nor the authority can unilaterally decide that an issue is resolved.

The voting window for a newly visible report runs for six hours by default. That length was chosen so cases do not sit open indefinitely, yet people who check the app during a normal working day still have time to respond. Government operators can adjust both the voting method and the window through configuration settings, so a live installation can lengthen or shorten the period without redeploying smart contracts. Every transition still has to pass the contract's own checks, and each step emits an event that can be reviewed later.

\section{Standard Report Workflow}
\label{sec:standard-report-workflow}

A standard report is processed as follows, and Figure~\ref{fig:report-submission-sequence} shows the same sequence across the identity simulator, relayer, AI aggregator, IPFS, and the Reporting contract.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,height=1.0\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/report-submission-sequence.png}
    \caption{Processing sequence for a standard citizen report}
    \label{fig:report-submission-sequence}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The citizen is checked by the identity simulator, and a signed single-use ticket is selected.
    \item The \ac{DApp} hashes the images, forms \(H_R\) from Eq.(\ref{eq:report-hash}), and signs it with the derived citizen wallet.
    \item The relayer verifies the government ticket signature, uploaded image hashes, and citizen signature. A pseudonym is derived using Eq.(\ref{eq:pseudonym}).
    \item A BullMQ flow is created. AI moderation is run first, before any storage or blockchain cost is spent, so that rejected content does not consume IPFS or gas resources. If it is accepted, the processed report is uploaded to IPFS. The CID and hash values are then submitted to the Reporting contract.
    \item The report is created as PendingValidation. The single-use ticket ID is stored as a submission nullifier.
    \item Community votes are accepted during the validation window. After finalization, the report becomes Open or CommunityRejected.
    \item An accepted report is handled by an authority worker. A solution or rejection is later reviewed by the community.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Pre-Submission Duplicate Detection and Similar Report Suggestions}
\label{sec:duplicate-detection-arch}

The system is designed to reduce duplicate reports and avoid redundant blockchain transaction fees. This is achieved via client-assisted pre-submission duplicate detection service. Before a report is submitted to the relayer queue, the client interface checks for existing active reports in the geographic vicinity of the targeted issue. Through this feature, the system can avoid overloading governance authorities with duplicate complaints. 

The detection workflow is described below. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item When a citizen selects a report category and adjusts the geographic location pin, the web application triggers a debounced (300\,ms) query to the relayer API endpoint   {/api/reports/check-duplicate}.
    \item The backend service queries the   {Reporting} smart contract to fetch recent active reports.
    \item Report metadata stored on \ac{IPFS} is fetched and parsed. To eliminate redundant network requests for immutable IPFS content, the server maintains an in-memory cache mapping CIDs to parsed metadata structures (category, description, coordinates).
    \item The backend calculates the great-circle distance between the user's coordinates $(lat_1, lng_1)$ and each existing report's coordinates $(lat_2, lng_2)$ using the Haversine formula given in Eq.(\ref{eq:haversine-a}) and Eq.(\ref{eq:haversine-d}). 
    \begin{equation}
        a = \sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta lat}{2}\right) + \cos(lat_1) \cdot \cos(lat_2) \cdot \sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta lng}{2}\right)
        \label{eq:haversine-a}
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        d = 2 \cdot R \cdot \operatorname{atan2}\left(\sqrt{a}, \sqrt{1-a}\right)
        \label{eq:haversine-d}
    \end{equation}
    where $R = 6{,}371\,\text{km}$ is the Earth's mean radius.
    \item  Candidate reports matching the user's selected category within a $1{,}000\,\text{meter}$ ($1\,\text{km}$) geofenced radius are flagged as potential duplicates.
    \item  If duplicates are detected, a modal dialog is rendered to the citizen displaying matching report IDs, categories, descriptions, and locations. The citizen can inspect existing active reports to determine if their issue is already undergoing civic validation or choose to proceed with submission if it represents a distinct new incident.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Emergency Report Workflow}
\label{sec:emergency-workflow}

Emergency reports use the same relayer checks, moderation pipeline, and IPFS storage process, so the same integrity and safety guarantees described above apply for them as well. However, a separate smart contract (EmergencyReporting) is used and a submitted emergency becomes Open directly rather than waiting for standard community validation, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:emergency-workflow}. This shortcut exists because the community voting window that protects the standard workflow from spam would be harmful for a genuine emergency, such as a gas leak or collapsing structure, where every additional hour of delay increases risk to public safety. Authorities may mark a case as in progress, close it as resolved, or downgrade it to a false emergency. In urgent situations they can record resolution straight from the Open state instead of waiting for an intermediate step, on the assumption that a genuine emergency may already be under control before the status is updated on the system.

The emergency route does not wait for community voting, which saves time but removes the spam filter that standard reports receive. The 30-day penalty box on emergency submissions is the compensating control. If an authority labels a report a false emergency, that citizen's pseudonym cannot file another emergency report through the Emergency Reporting contract for thirty days. Honest users still get a short, direct path when they need it. Repeated false alarms become expensive. The restriction targets only the emergency contract, so the same person can continue to raise ordinary reports while the timer runs.

\label{sec:community-voting}

Standard reports pass through three points where the community is asked to vote.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initial validation
    \item Solution verification
    \item Authority-rejection review
\end{enumerate}
Table~\ref{tab:voting-phases} summarizes the outcome of each phase, and Figures~\ref{fig:community-validation-workflow} and~\ref{fig:post-resolution-workflow} show the underlying vote-processing steps.

Each vote requires its own government-signed ticket that can be spent only once. Before forwarding the vote, the relayer validates that ticket together with the citizen's signature on the vote payload. The ticket identifier is recorded as a nullifier so the same ticket cannot be reused, and the pseudonym is checked so a citizen cannot cast more than one vote in the same phase.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth,height=1.0\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/community-validation-workflow.png}

    \label{fig:community-validation-workflow}

By default, a vote passes when approvals outnumber rejections. The Reporting contract can also be switched to a two-thirds supermajority, a minimum turnout threshold, or a hybrid rule that combines two criteria. There is no single rule that every local governance body would accept. What works for one council may be too weak or too slow for another. Choosing how votes are finalised is therefore left to the deploying authority, and the system implements all of the options listed above. After a method is selected and applied, it governs every future standard-report voting window across the deployment.

Expired windows can be finalized by lazy evaluation when a late vote is received, or by batch finalization using scheduled cron jobs. Providing two ways to trigger finalization means that a report's progress does not depend on any single actor remembering to close its voting window. A citizen's late vote can trigger it, or a scheduled cron job can trigger it. 

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering

For validation, a positive result changes PendingValidation to Open, otherwise it changes to CommunityRejected. For solution verification, acceptance changes PendingVerification to Closed, rejection changes it to Reopened. For rejection review, an upheld rejection changes PendingRejectionReview to Closed, a successful appeal changes it to Reopened.

\section{Authority Workflow}
\label{sec:authority-workflow}

An authorized worker uses MetaMask to act on reports, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:authority-workflow}. An Open or Reopened report can be claimed and changed to InProgress. Progress comments and optional image CIDs can be appended without changing the status. A completed action is submitted with evidence and changes the report to PendingVerification. An invalid report can be changed to PendingRejectionReview.

    \centering

    \caption{Authority action and multisig governance workflow}
    \label{fig:authority-workflow}
\end{figure}

Authority access is governed by the AuthorityMultiSig contract. Super Admins submit and vote on proposals to add, remove government authorities. A strict majority is required to approve a proposal, as given in Eq.(\ref{eq:governance-quorum}).

\begin{equation}
    Q = \left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor + 1,
    \label{eq:governance-quorum}
\end{equation}

where \(Q\) is the required number of approvals and \(N\) is the current number of Super Admins. A proposal needs more than half of the group to vote yes, not just one signature or the largest share of votes cast. That bar blocks a single stolen or misused Super Admin account from registering a malicious authority worker, stripping a real one, or rewriting the voting rules on its own. The threshold follows the same idea as conventional multisignature admin panels, where sensitive changes need several independent sign-offs.

Once passed, a proposal may add or remove Super Admins, add or remove authority workers, or update the voting method on the Reporting contract. When both standard and emergency contract addresses are wired in, worker grants and revocations are written to each, so the same person does not end up authorised on one channel and blocked on the other.

\section{Opinion Polling}
\label{sec:opinion-polling}

The OpinionPolling contract is used for public consultation, and it is kept as its own contract rather than folded into Reporting because a poll follows a much simpler lifecycle than a civic issue report. It needs a deadline and a tally, but no community validation, authority assignment, or multi-step resolution. Keeping it separate lets the polling feature evolve independently and mirrors the same separation-of-concerns reasoning already used to give emergencies their own contract.

An authorized authority creates a poll with an IPFS metadata CID, a future deadline, and either a true/false or multi-choice type. A citizen vote contains the poll ID, option index, and a single-use nullifier. The contract rejects a reused nullifier for the same poll and increases the selected option count. Figure~\ref{fig:opinion-polling-workflow} shows the full workflow from poll creation to finalization.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth,height=1.0\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/opinion-polling-workflow.png}
    \caption{Public opinion polling workflow}

Tallies are visible to everyone, but nothing in the vote record ties a vote back to a named citizen. The chain stores only the poll ID, the selected option, and a nullifier that cannot be spent twice on the same poll. Citizens therefore take part through the same ticket-and-nullifier pattern used for reports and community votes, gaining a public say without leaving an identity trail next to their choice. When the deadline passes and finalization runs, the poll is closed and the outcome is fixed on the ledger.

\section{Design Rationale}
\label{sec:design-rationale}

A private Clique \ac{PoA} network is used instead of a public blockchain because block confirmation can complete in seconds when only a small, pre-approved validator set is involved. That responsiveness matters when citizens expect a report or vote to show up without a long delay. Rather than the longer waits typical on open networks. A non-mining RPC node separates application access from block sealing for the reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:layered-architecture}. Read and write traffic should never be able to interfere with consensus.

The four-contract design separates normal reports, urgent reports, governance, and public opinion consultation because each has a different lifecycle and a different risk. Combining them into a single contract would force every report, including a routine pothole complaint, to carry the complexity needed for emergency fast-tracking and poll tallying, making the contract harder to reason and to test. Splitting them keeps each contract's logic close to the one workflow it serves.

The relayer is used because citizens should be able to participate without first acquiring cryptocurrency or understanding gas fees, both of which are significant adoption barriers for a public-service tool that needs to reach non-technical users. BullMQ is used because AI content moderation, IPFS storage, and blockchain confirmation can together take longer time than a single HTTP request should take. 
Work is handed off through an ordered queue so a failed moderation call or a slow IPFS upload can be retried on its own, without replaying steps that already completed. Large attachments stay in \ac{IPFS} rather than inside contract storage, because putting even one photograph on-chain would inflate gas for every node that holds a copy of the ledger. The on-chain record keeps only the \ac{CID}, which is enough for anyone to fetch the file later and confirm it still matches what was submitted.

Identity is handled by a simulator that hands out signed, single-use tickets instead of building a full decentralised identity stack, which sits outside this project's stated scope. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} outlines how that layer, along with stronger auditing and a wider validator governance model, could be extended in future work.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:architecture-summary}

This chapter laid out a design built in layers. Simulated identity checks, relayed submission, \ac{AI} moderation, \ac{IPFS} storage, and a private \ac{PoA} ledger each handle a separate concern. Standard reports must clear two community votes, one before an authority acts and one after a fix is claimed, so neither side can close a case on its own. Emergency reports skip the first vote to save time, with a thirty-day penalty box to discourage abuse of that faster path.
Multisignature governance keeps authority access and voting configuration under shared control rather than any single account. Pseudonymous tickets give citizens a practical, working form of participation that does not require them to reveal their identity in order to submit a report, vote, or take part in a public opinion poll. Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} explains how each of these design decisions was implemented, and Chapters~\ref{ch:testing} and~\ref{ch:results} evaluate how well the resulting prototype performs against them.
