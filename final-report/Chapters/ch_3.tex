\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:architecture-introduction}

This chapter presents the design of the local governance reporting system. The system is divided into clear layers. Identity checking, content processing, storage, and public record keeping are separated, with each responsibility handled by the part of the system best suited to it. Facts that must be trusted by every participant and never quietly altered, such as a report's status, a vote count, or an authority's action, are recorded on a private blockchain. Work that is heavy or slow, such as storing photographs or running \ac{AI} moderation models, is done off-chain, and only its result and a verifiable hash are anchored on-chain. This split keeps the ledger small and fast while still making every important decision auditable.

The design supports standard civic reports, emergency reports, community voting, authority work, and public opinion polls. Figure~\ref{fig:component-interaction} gives a high-level view of how the main components communicate before the rest of the chapter explains each part and the reasoning behind it. Citizen participation is pseudonymous on-chain. A citizen authenticates through a government identity check and receives signed, single-use tickets, consistent with the identity-simulation scope adopted for this prototype. The sections that follow explain how these tickets support anonymous but accountable participation throughout the reporting, voting, and polling workflows.

\section{Overall Layered Architecture}
\label{sec:layered-architecture}

A hybrid on-chain and off-chain design is used because the two environments are capable at different tasks. The blockchain is well suited to recording facts that must be trusted by every participant and never silently altered, such as status of a report or a vote tally. However, writing large media files or running machine learning inference directly on-chain would be time consuming and expensive to replicate across every node. The system offers both auditability and practical performance by keeping heavy work off-chain, while anchoring only its outcome and a verifiable hash on-chain. 
\begin{enumerate}
     \item On-Chain Environment:  Executed on a private, permissioned \ac{PoA} network. It maintains minimal, immutable state variables. It includes report metadata, opinion polling metadata, lifecycle state transitions, cryptographic content hashes, single-use ticket nullifiers,\ac{RBAC} configurations, multi-signature governance proposals, and community voting tallies.
    \item Off-Chain Environment: Comprises data-intensive and computationally heavy microservices, including multimedia storage on \ac{IPFS}, automated \ac{AI} content moderation, gasless transaction orchestration via a NestJS middleware relayer, automated wallet funding, and scheduled background cron jobs for voting window finalization.
\end{enumerate}

These layer boundaries are drawn along trust and technology. The identity simulator is decoupled from the relayer because it represents a different administrative authority. AI and IPFS services are comparatively slow and are best isolated behind asynchronous jobs rather than called synchronously. Consequently, the relayer and queue layer are maintained in a seperated location from AI and IPFS. The blockchain layer is kept as thin as possible because every extra byte stored or computation executed on-chain is replicated and paid for by every node in the network. Therefore, only the facts that genuinely need a tamper-evident public record are stored on blockchain.

The blockchain uses chain ID 1337 and a 5 $s$ Clique period. Three validator nodes seal blocks, and a fourth node does not mine but instead exposes the \ac{JSON}-\ac{RPC} interface used by the rest of the system, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:validator-topology}. A 5 $s$ block period is short enough to give citizens fast confirmation feedback for a civic reporting workload, while still being long enough for three validators to agree reliably without specialised hardware. Separating the \ac{RPC}-only node from the three sealing validators means that read traffic from the dApp and write traffic from the relayer can never compete with or accidentally disrupt the block-sealing process. If the RPC node is restarted or briefly overloaded block production continues unaffected on the other three nodes. Direct reads are made by the dApp through this RPC interface. Citizen writes are normally sent through the relayer. Authority transactions are signed with MetaMask.

\section{Trust Boundaries}
\label{sec:trust-boundaries}

The designed system contains multiple trust boundaries. Explicitly identifying these boundaries, rather than treating the system as one uniformly trusted entity clarifies which component must behave correctly for a given guarantee to hold. This distinction is essential for undersanding what the  blockchain's tamper-evidence does and does not ensure. 


\begin{enumerate}
    \item Citizen device boundary: The citizen seed, derived wallet, ticket batch, and optional encrypted session are handled in the browser. A compromised browser can expose these values.
    \item Identity simulator boundary: The simulator sees the government ID and password. It is trusted to check the simulated citizen record and to issue valid tickets only after successful authentication.
    \item Relayer boundary: The relayer sees report text, location, images, ticket data, and the citizen wallet address. It is trusted to verify requests and to submit only approved data.
    \item AI boundary: The aggregator and classifier services receive report content. Their decisions are advisory off-chain gates and are not blockchain consensus.
    \item Storage boundary: The IPFS service receives the accepted complaint envelope. No authentication is implemented at the service level. Network isolation is therefore assumed.
    \item Blockchain boundary: Validators are trusted collectively to operate the private network. Contract rules are shared by all nodes, but the permissioned validator group is not fully trustless.
\end{enumerate}

These boundaries show that blockchain integrity does not remove trust from identity checking, moderation, storage access, or endpoint operation.

\section{Privacy and Security Design}
\label{sec:privacy-security-design}

Citizen identity is decoupled from the public report record through a government identity check. A government ID and password are verified by the identity simulator, and government-signed, single-use ticket IDs are issued only after this check succeeds. Each ticket behave as a nullifier. The nullifier gives evidence that one unit of participation was issued to an eligible citizen, and the contract's nullifier mapping stops that same ticket from being used a second time. This ticket-based approach is used because it lets the prototype enforce single-action-per-eligible-citizen immediately using well-understood signature and mapping checks. This approach preserves the possibility of introducing a stronger anonymous-credential or \ac{ZKP}  system at a later stage, without requiring to modify how the relayer, the contracts, or the voting logic consume tickets.

% \begin{figure}[!htb]
%     \centering
%     \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth,height=0.6\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/authentication-workflow.png}
%     \caption{Citizen authentication, ticket issuance, and pseudonym derivation}
%     \label{fig:authentication-workflow}
% \end{figure}

A citizen wallet is derived deterministically from a citizen seed in the browser. The relayer derives an on-chain pseudonym as given in Eq.~\ref{eq:pseudonym}.

\begin{equation}
    P = \operatorname{keccak256}\left(A \parallel D\right),
    \label{eq:pseudonym}
\end{equation}

where \(P\) is the citizen pseudonym, \(A\) is the citizen wallet address, \(D\) is a configured domain salt, and \(\parallel\) denotes packed concatenation. This design keeps the citizen's wallet address out of public report records, while still letting the contract recognise that two submissions came from the same citizen through the stable pseudonym. That recognition is a deliberate design choice rather than an oversight. It is exactly what allows the one-vote-per-citizen and one-report-per-ticket rules described later in this chapter to be enforced without the contract ever learning who the citizen is. The result is pseudonymous participation which a practical and useful privacy-preserving level for a prototype. Furthermore, it provides a foundation that a future anonymous-credential scheme could build on and strengthen further.

Before a report is queued, the relayer verifies the government signature on the ticket, the hash of every uploaded image, and the citizen's \ac{ECDSA} signature on the report payload, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:relayer-verification}. These checks are deliberately ordered from cheapest to most expensive. The government ticket signature is checked first because it is the fastest way to discard a request that was never properly authorized, before spending time on validating image hashes or recovering the signer of the heavier report-hash signature.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth, keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/relayer-verification.png}
    \caption{Relayer dual-signature verification pipeline}
    \label{fig:relayer-verification}
\end{figure}

The \ac{AI} aggregator is protected by an \ac{API} key, a relayer signature, a timestamp limit, and a nonce replay store. A missing classifier response or a failed classifier request is treated as a rejection. This fail-closed rule is chosen over a fail-open alternative because the cost of the two error types is not symmetric. A citizen whose report is delayed by a temporary moderation outage can simply resubmit once the service recovers. Whereas content that bypasses moderation because a classifier unavailability cannot  subsequently be removed from the tamper-evident ledger. Treating uncertainty as a rejection protects the harder-to-reverse outcome.

\section{Report Data and Composite Hash}
\label{sec:report-data-hash}

The complete public report content is not stored in a smart contract due to storage and gas cost constraints. The IPFS complaint envelope contains the description, category, location, image count, image metadata, base64-encoded image data, and storage time. The blockchain record contains the IPFS CID, report hash, submission nullifier, citizen pseudonym, timestamps, status, vote counters, assigned authority, and latest authority evidence.

Each image \(I_i\) is hashed by the client and checked by the relayer as given in Eq.~\ref{eq:image-hash}.

\begin{equation}
    h_i = \operatorname{keccak256}(I_i).
    \label{eq:image-hash}
\end{equation}

The ordered image hashes are joined as \(H_I=h_1\parallel h_2\parallel\cdots\parallel h_n\). Hashing every image individually and then combining the hashes in the order the citizen uploaded them means that a single signature over the final report hash can protect the whole submission. If any image were swapped, dropped, or reordered after the citizen signed, the recomputed hash would no longer match and the relayer would reject it. The signed report hash is given by Eq.~\ref{eq:report-hash}.

\begin{equation}
    H_R = \operatorname{keccak256}\left(d \parallel t \parallel H_I\right),
    \label{eq:report-hash}
\end{equation}

where \(H_R\) is the composite report hash, \(d\) is the report description, \(t\) is the one-use ticket ID, and \(H_I\) is the ordered image-hash string. The citizen signs \(H_R\). The relayer reconstructs it and recovers the signer address.

Two separate community checkpoints are built into this lifecycle on purpose. The first, PendingValidation, protects authority time by filtering out spam, duplicates, and clearly invalid reports before any government worker is asked to act on them. The second, PendingVerification, protects citizens by making sure an authority's claim of a fix is independently confirmed by the people affected before a report is closed, rather than letting an authority close its own work unchecked. Together, the two checkpoints ensure that neither the community nor the authority can unilaterally decide that an issue is resolved.

The default voting window is six hours, which is short enough to keep the workflow responsive while still giving citizens across a working day a reasonable chance to see and vote on a new report. The voting method and window can be changed through governmente side configuration. As a result, a deployment can tune this balance without changing contract code. State changes are checked by contract functions, and events provide an audit trail.

\section{Standard Report Workflow}
\label{sec:standard-report-workflow}

A standard report is processed as follows, and Figure~\ref{fig:report-submission-sequence} shows the same sequence across the identity simulator, relayer, AI aggregator, IPFS, and the Reporting contract.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,height=1.0\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/report-submission-sequence.png}
    \caption{Processing sequence for a standard citizen report}
    \label{fig:report-submission-sequence}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The citizen is checked by the identity simulator, and a signed single-use ticket is selected.
    \item The dApp hashes the images, forms \(H_R\) from Eq.~\ref{eq:report-hash}, and signs it with the derived citizen wallet.
    \item The relayer verifies the government ticket signature, uploaded image hashes, and citizen signature. A pseudonym is derived using Eq.~\ref{eq:pseudonym}.
    \item A BullMQ flow is created. AI moderation is run first, before any storage or blockchain cost is spent, so that rejected contentdoes not consume IPFS or gas resources. If it is accepted, the processed report is uploaded to IPFS. The CID and hash values are then submitted to the Reporting contract.
    \item The report is created as PendingValidation. The single-use ticket ID is stored as a submission nullifier.
    \item Community votes are accepted during the validation window. After finalization, the report becomes Open or CommunityRejected.
    \item An accepted report is handled by an authority worker. A solution or rejection is later reviewed by the community.
\end{enumerate}


Figure~\ref{fig:ai-moderation-flow} shows how the three classifiers and the aggregator reach the accept-or-reject decision referenced in step 4.

\label{sec:emergency-workflow}

This shortcut exists because the community voting window that protects the standard workflow from spam would be harmful for a genuine emergency, such as a gas leak or collapsing structure, where every additional hour of delay increases risk to public safety. The default method is a simple majority, where the positive count must be greater than the negative count. The contract also supports a two-thirds supermajority, a minimum-vote threshold, and a hybrid of two methods. These options exist because not every decision carries the same weight. A  simple majority is adequate for the everyday validation of routine reports, while for issues that require stronger validation, another voting method can be configured.

Expired windows can be finalized by lazy evaluation when a late vote is received, or by batch finalization using scheduled cron jobs. Providing two ways to trigger finalization means that a report's progress does not depend on any single actor remembering to close its voting window. A citizen's late vote can trigger it, or a scheduled cron job can trigger it.

For validation, a positive result changes PendingValidation to Open, otherwise it changes to CommunityRejected. For solution verification, acceptance changes PendingVerification to Closed, rejection changes it to Reopened. For rejection review, an upheld rejection changes PendingRejectionReview to Closed, a successful appeal changes it to Reopened.

\section{Authority Workflow}
\label{sec:authority-workflow}

An authorized worker uses MetaMask to act on reports, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:authority-workflow}. An Open or Reopened report can be claimed and changed to InProgress. Progress comments and optional image CIDs can be appended without changing the status. A completed action is submitted with evidence and changes the report to PendingVerification. An invalid report can be changed to PendingRejectionReview.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/authority-workflow.png}
    \caption{Authority action and multisig governance workflow}
    \label{fig:authority-workflow}
\end{figure}

Authority access is governed by the AuthorityMultiSig contract. Super Admins submit and vote on proposals to add, remove government authorities. A strict majority is required to approve a proposal, as given in Eq.~\ref{eq:governance-quorum}.

\begin{equation}
    Q = \left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor + 1,
    \label{eq:governance-quorum}
\end{equation}

where \(Q\) is the required number of approvals and \(N\) is the current number of Super Admins. Requiring a strict majority, rather than a simple plurality or a single approver, ensures that no single compromised or rogue Super Admin account can unilaterally add a fake authority worker, remove a legitimate one, or change the voting method. At least half of the Super Admin group must independently agree, which mirrors how sensitive administrative changes are handled in traditional multi-signature governance designs.

Approved proposals can add or remove Super Admins, add or remove authority workers, and change the Reporting contract voting method. Authority changes are applied to both standard and emergency reporting when both contract references are configured, so that a worker's permissions stay consistent everywhere. 

\section{Opinion Polling}
\label{sec:opinion-polling}

The OpinionPolling contract is used for public consultation, and it is kept as its own contract rather than folded into Reporting because a poll follows a much simpler lifecycle than a civic issue report. It needs a deadline and a tally, but no community validation, authority assignment, or multi-step resolution. Keeping it separate lets the polling feature evolve independently and mirrors the same separation-of-concerns reasoning already used to give emergencies their own contract.

An authorized authority creates a poll with an IPFS metadata CID, a future deadline, and either a true/false or multi-choice type. A citizen vote contains the poll ID, option index, and a single-use nullifier. The contract rejects a reused nullifier for the same poll and increases the selected option count. Figure~\ref{fig:opinion-polling-workflow} shows the full workflow from poll creation to finalization.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth,height=1.0\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Images/Chapter 3/opinion-polling-workflow.png}
    \caption{Public opinion polling workflow}
    \label{fig:opinion-polling-workflow}
\end{figure}

Poll results are public counts, and their identity details are never stored alongside a vote. Only the poll ID, the chosen option, and a single-use nullifier are recorded. This gives citizens a low-friction, ticket-based way to make their opinion count publicly without exposing who cast which vote, using the same nullifier mechanism already trusted elsewhere in the system for reports and validation votes. A poll is marked inactive and results are published when it is finalized.

\section{Design Rationale}
\label{sec:design-rationale}

The private Clique PoA network is selected over a public chain because a small, known set of validators can agree on blocks in seconds rather than minutes, which matters for a citizen-facing reporting workflow. A non-mining RPC node separates application access from block sealing for the reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:layered-architecture}, read and write traffic should never be able to interfere with consensus.

The four-contract design separates normal reports, urgent reports, governance, and public opinion consultation because each has a different lifecycle and a different risk. Combining them into a single contract would force every report, including a routine pothole complaint, to carry the complexity needed for emergency fast-tracking and poll tallying, making the contract harder to reason and to test. Splitting them keeps each contract's logic close to the one workflow it serves.

The relayer is used because citizens should be able to participate without first acquiring cryptocurrency or understanding gas fees, both of which are significant adoption barriers for a public-service tool that needs to reach non-technical users. BullMQ is used because AI content moderation, IPFS storage, and blockchain confirmation can together take longer time than a single HTTP request should take. An ordered job queue lets each step retry independently without repeating steps that already succeeded. IPFS is used to keep large content outside smart contracts, since storing a photograph directly on-chain would multiply gas cost for every validator that replicates the ledger. A \ac{CID} still lets anyone verify that the off-chain content matches what was originally submitted.

The identity layer is built as a simulator that issues signed, single-use tickets rather than a fully custom identity system as mentioned in the scope of the project. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} discusses how this identity layer, together with independent auditing and broader validator governance, can be strengthened as the project matures.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:architecture-summary}

This chapter has presented a layered design in which identity simulation, relayed processing, AI moderation, IPFS storage, and private PoA records each play a distinct, well-justified role. Standard reports pass through two community checkpoints so that neither the public nor the authority can unilaterally decide that an issue is resolved. Emergencies are opened immediately to keep urgent response fast, backed by a penalty-box safeguard against misuse of that shortcut. Multisignature governance keeps authority access and voting configuration under shared control rather than any single account. Pseudonymous tickets give citizens a practical, working form of participation that does not require them to reveal their identity in order to submit a report, vote, or take part in a public opinion poll. Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} explains how each of these design decisions was implemented, and Chapters~\ref{ch:testing} and~\ref{ch:results} evaluate how well the resulting prototype performs against them.
