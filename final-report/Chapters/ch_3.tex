\chapter{System Architecture and Workflow Design}
\label{ch:architecture}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:architecture-introduction}

This chapter presents the design of the local governance reporting system. The system is divided into clear layers so that identity checking, content processing, storage, and public record keeping are separated. Large files and machine learning tasks are processed off-chain. Important report states, votes, authority actions, and governance decisions are recorded on a private blockchain.

The design supports standard civic reports, emergency reports, community voting, authority work, and public opinion polls. Citizen participation is pseudonymous on-chain. However, the identity component is a simulator and not a real zero-knowledge proof system as mentioned in the scope of the project.

\section{Overall Layered Architecture}
\label{sec:layered-architecture}

A hybrid on-chain and off-chain design is used. 

\begin{enumerate}
     \item On-Chain Environment:  Executed on a private, permissioned \ac{PoA} network. It maintains minimal, immutable state variables, including report metadata, opinion polling metadata, lifecycle state transitions, cryptographic content hashes, single-use ticket nullifiers,\ac{RBAC} configurations, multi-signature governance proposals, and community voting tallies.
    \item Off-Chain Environment: Comprises data-intensive and computationally heavy microservices, including multimedia storage on \ac{IPFS}, automated multi-classifier AI content moderation, gasless transaction orchestration via a NestJS middleware relayer, automated wallet funding, and scheduled background cron jobs for voting window finalization.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Trust Boundaries}
\label{sec:trust-boundaries}

The design contains several trust boundaries. 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Citizen device boundary: The citizen seed, derived wallet, ticket batch, and optional encrypted session are handled in the browser. A compromised browser can expose these values.
    \item Identity simulator boundary: The simulator sees the government ID and password. It is trusted to check the simulated citizen record and to issue valid tickets only after successful authentication.
    \item Relayer boundary: The relayer sees report text, location, images, ticket data, and the citizen wallet address. It is trusted to verify requests and to submit only approved data.
    \item AI boundary: The aggregator and classifier services receive report content. Their decisions are advisory off-chain gates and are not blockchain consensus.
    \item Storage boundary: The IPFS service receives the accepted complaint envelope. No authentication is implemented at the service level. Network isolation is therefore assumed.
    \item Blockchain boundary: Validators are trusted collectively to operate the private network. Contract rules are shared by all nodes, but the permissioned validator group is not fully trustless.
\end{enumerate}

These boundaries show that blockchain integrity does not remove trust from identity checking, moderation, storage access, or endpoint operation.

\section{Privacy and Security Design}
\label{sec:privacy-security-design}

Citizen identity is not protected by a simulated government identity service. A government ID and password are checked, and government-signed, single-use ticket IDs are issued. These ticket IDs serve as nullifiers. They provide proof that a ticket was issued and prevent repeated use after it is recorded. They do not provide a cryptographic proof of identity attributes without revealing the authentication process. A true zero-knowledge credential system is future work.

A citizen wallet is derived deterministically from a citizen seed in the browser. The relayer derives an on-chain pseudonym as given in Eq.~\ref{eq:pseudonym}.

\begin{equation}
    P = \operatorname{keccak256}\left(A \parallel D\right),
    \label{eq:pseudonym}
\end{equation}

where \(P\) is the citizen pseudonym, \(A\) is the citizen wallet address, \(D\) is a configured domain salt, and \(\parallel\) denotes packed concatenation. This design hides the raw address from contract report records, but the stable pseudonym allows reports from the same derived wallet to be linked. It therefore provides pseudonymity rather than full anonymity.

Before a report is queued, the relayer verifies the government signature on the ticket, the hash of every uploaded image, and the citizen's \ac{ECDSA} signature on the report payload. The \ac{AI} aggregator is protected by an \ac{API} key, a relayer signature, a timestamp limit, and a nonce replay store. A missing classifier response or a failed classifier request is treated as a rejection. This fail-closed rule reduces the chance that unavailable moderation services are bypassed.

\section{Report Data and Composite Hash}
\label{sec:report-data-hash}

The complete public report content is not stored in a smart contract due to storage and gas cost constraints. The IPFS complaint envelope contains the description, category, location, image count, image metadata, base64-encoded image data, and storage time. The blockchain record contains the IPFS CID, report hash, submission nullifier, citizen pseudonym, timestamps, status, vote counters, assigned authority, and latest authority evidence.

Each image \(I_i\) is hashed by the client and checked by the relayer as given in Eq.~\ref{eq:image-hash}.

\begin{equation}
    h_i = \operatorname{keccak256}(I_i).
    \label{eq:image-hash}
\end{equation}

The ordered image hashes are joined as \(H_I=h_1\parallel h_2\parallel\cdots\parallel h_n\). The signed report hash is given by Eq.~\ref{eq:report-hash}.

\begin{equation}
    H_R = \operatorname{keccak256}\left(d \parallel t \parallel H_I\right),
    \label{eq:report-hash}
\end{equation}

where \(H_R\) is the composite report hash, \(d\) is the report description, \(t\) is the one-use ticket ID, and \(H_I\) is the ordered image-hash string. The citizen signs \(H_R\). The relayer reconstructs it and recovers the signer address.

\section{Standard Report Workflow}
\label{sec:standard-report-workflow}

A standard report is processed as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The citizen is checked by the identity simulator, and a signed one-use ticket is selected.
    \item The dApp hashes the images, forms \(H_R\) from Eq.~\ref{eq:report-hash}, and signs it with the derived citizen wallet.
    \item The relayer verifies the government ticket signature, uploaded image hashes, and citizen signature. A pseudonym is derived using Eq.~\ref{eq:pseudonym}.
    \item A BullMQ flow is created. AI moderation is run first. If it is accepted, the processed report is uploaded to IPFS. The CID and hash values are then submitted to the Reporting contract.
    \item The report is created as \texttt{PendingValidation}. The one-use ticket ID is stored as a submission nullifier.
    \item Community votes are accepted during the validation window. After finalization, the report becomes \texttt{Open} or \texttt{CommunityRejected}.
    \item An accepted report is handled by an authority worker. A solution or rejection is later reviewed by the community.
\end{enumerate}

Queue progress is streamed to the citizen through \ac{SSE}. Events are filtered by the citizen wallet address so that the active client receives its own job updates.

\section{Emergency Report Workflow}
\label{sec:emergency-workflow}

Emergency reports use the same relayer checks, moderation pipeline, and IPFS storage process. However, the EmergencyReporting contract is used. A submitted emergency becomes \texttt{Open} directly and does not wait for standard community validation.

Four emergency states are used: \texttt{Open}, \texttt{InProgress}, \texttt{Resolved}, and \texttt{Reclassified}. An authority can start work, resolve an emergency, or reclassify a false emergency. Resolution can be made directly from \texttt{Open} when a rapid response is needed. When an emergency is reclassified, its citizen pseudonym is placed in a 30-day emergency penalty box. This control applies only to the emergency channel.

\section{Community Voting}
\label{sec:community-voting}

Community voting is used in three standard-report phases: initial validation, solution verification, and authority-rejection review. A separate one-use government-signed ticket is supplied for each vote. The relayer checks the ticket and the citizen signature. The ticket ID is used as a vote nullifier, while the pseudonym is also checked to enforce one vote per citizen in the relevant phase.

The default method is a simple majority, where the positive count must be greater than the negative count. The contract also supports a two-thirds supermajority, a minimum-vote threshold, and a hybrid of two methods. Expired windows can be finalized by a direct call, by lazy evaluation when a late vote is received, or by batch finalization.

For validation, a positive result changes \texttt{PendingValidation} to \texttt{Open}; otherwise it changes to \texttt{CommunityRejected}. For solution verification, acceptance changes \texttt{PendingVerification} to \texttt{Closed}; rejection changes it to \texttt{Reopened}. For rejection review, an upheld rejection changes \texttt{PendingRejectionReview} to \texttt{Closed}; a successful appeal changes it to \texttt{Reopened}.

\section{Authority Workflow}
\label{sec:authority-workflow}

An authorized worker uses MetaMask to act on reports. An \texttt{Open} or \texttt{Reopened} report can be claimed and changed to \texttt{InProgress}. Progress comments and optional image CIDs can be appended without changing the status. A completed action is submitted with evidence and changes the report to \texttt{PendingVerification}. An invalid report can be changed to \texttt{PendingRejectionReview}.

Authority access is governed by the AuthorityMultiSig contract. Super Admins submit and vote on proposals. A strict majority is required:

\begin{equation}
    Q = \left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor + 1,
    \label{eq:governance-quorum}
\end{equation}

where \(Q\) is the required number of approvals and \(N\) is the current number of Super Admins. Approved proposals can add or remove Super Admins, add or remove authority workers, and change the Reporting contract voting method. Authority changes are applied to both standard and emergency reporting when both contract references are configured.

Optional Planka workforce tracking can mirror report states to task cards. It is feature-flagged and is not required for the on-chain workflow.

\section{Opinion Polling}
\label{sec:opinion-polling}

The OpinionPolling contract is used for public consultation. An authorized authority creates a poll with an IPFS metadata CID, a future deadline, and either a true/false or multi-choice type. A citizen vote contains the poll ID, option index, and a one-use nullifier. The contract rejects a reused nullifier for the same poll and increases the selected option count.

Poll results are public counts, while government identity details are not stored with votes. This is pseudonymous ticket-based voting, not a zero-knowledge voting protocol. A poll is marked inactive when it is finalized.

\section{Design Rationale}
\label{sec:design-rationale}

The private Clique PoA network is selected because known validators and short block periods are suitable for a controlled final-year prototype. A non-mining RPC node separates application access from block sealing. The four-contract design separates normal reports, urgent reports, governance, and consultation.

The relayer is used because citizens are not required to manage gas or send direct blockchain transactions. BullMQ is used because AI inference, IPFS storage, and blockchain confirmation can take longer than one HTTP request. IPFS is used to keep large content outside smart contracts, while a CID preserves content addressing.

The identity simulator is used to test eligibility and one-use tickets without integration with a real national identity provider. It must not be treated as a production privacy system. A real anonymous credential or zero-knowledge proof design, independent audits, stronger key recovery, authenticated storage endpoints, and a wider validator governance model are left for future work.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:architecture-summary}

This chapter defines a layered design for identity simulation, relayed processing, AI moderation, IPFS storage, and private PoA records. Standard reports are community validated through eight states, while emergencies are opened directly. Multisig governance controls authority access and voting configuration. The design provides pseudonymous participation and one-use ticket nullifiers, but it does not provide production-grade zero knowledge or a decentralized oracle network.
