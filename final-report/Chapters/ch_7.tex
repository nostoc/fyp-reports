\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:conclusion-introduction}

This chapter concludes the report and summarizes the implemented system, relates the main findings to the evidence recorded in Chapters~\ref{ch:testing} and~\ref{ch:results}, states the project's contributions, and outlines the future work that would take the prototype from a validated proof of concept toward a broader real-world deployment. The conclusions drawn here are grounded in the prototype and evaluation described in Chapters~\ref{ch:architecture}--\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Summary of the Implemented System}
\label{sec:implemented-system-summary}

This project has produced a working blockchain-based, community-assisted, reporting framework for local governance. Its blockchain layer is a private Geth Clique \ac{PoA} network with chain ID 1337, three validator nodes, and one non-mining RPC node. Four smart contracts,   {Reporting},   {EmergencyReporting},   {OpinionPolling}, and   {AuthorityMultiSig}, together enforce standard reports, emergency reports, community validation and post-resolution verification, authority actions, opinion polls, and governed authority access.

Citizen writes are processed by a relayer, which verifies government-signed tickets and citizen signatures before handing accepted requests to a Redis-backed BullMQ pipeline. AI moderation, IPFS upload, and blockchain submission are carried out in order within this pipeline. The progress is streamed back to the citizen through \ac{SSE} so that the multi-step process remains visible rather than a single opaque wait. Accepted report data and images are stored in one IPFS JSON envelope, while its CID and related hashes are recorded on-chain. A Next.js dApp serves citizens and authorities, and a separate portal serves the government identity simulator.

The AI layer combines safety, spam, and civic-relevance classifier services behind a fail-closed aggregator, so that moderation decisions are made before content is anchored on the tamper-evident ledger. Citizen activity is represented on-chain by stable pseudonyms, and government-signed single-use tickets act as nullifiers that let the contracts enforce one action per eligible citizen without ever learning who that citizen is. This ticket-and-nullifier interface is deliberately kept swappable, so that a stronger anonymous-credential or zero-knowledge protocol could take the place of the current identity simulator in a later iteration without requiring changes to the relayer, the contracts, or the voting logic that consumes tickets.

\section{Key Findings Against the Evidence}
\label{sec:conclusion-findings}

The evidence recorded in Chapter~\ref{ch:testing} and interpreted in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} supports the project's central goal. A permissioned blockchain and contract-controlled civic workflow has been designed, implemented, and verified end to end. All 75 smart-contract cases across the   {Reporting},   {EmergencyReporting},   {OpinionPolling},   {AuthorityMultiSig}, and   {Security} suites pass, together with all 25 relayer unit-test cases, all 20 AI oracle cases, and all 8 IPFS integration cases, for a combined 128 passing test cases. A dedicated benchmark on the live private network recorded an average of 309{,}056 gas per   {submitReport()}, a 5{,}017\,ms average submit-to-confirm latency, 0.87 TPS on a 20-transaction batch, and a 5.0\,s average Clique block interval, values that comfortably suit a civic-reporting workload once gas payment is handled transparently by the relayer. One complete report lifecycle has also been exercised from simulated identity issuance through community validation, authority resolution, and post-resolution verification to closure, confirming that the individually tested components genuinely compose into the intended workflow.

The interface goal has likewise been achieved at prototype level. Citizen and authority interfaces are implemented and were exercised throughout the end-to-end scenario, gas handling is hidden behind the relayer, and progress is shown through SSE rather than a silent wait.

The privacy and AI goals have been achieved to the level intended for this prototype stage. Government identity data is never written to the contracts. Only pseudonyms, ticket hashes, and cryptographic proofs appear on-chain, and this design is precisely what the passing Security suite exercises through its replay and duplicate-vote checks. The three AI classifier services and their fail-closed aggregator correctly separated accepted civic content from hate speech, spam, off-topic text, and unsafe images across all recorded test cases. These results establish a practical, tested foundation for pseudonymous participation and pre-anchoring content moderation. 

Recorded timing and throughput values show that the system responds well within the expectations of a controlled prototype deployment. Extending this evidence with wider repetition counts, committed raw benchmark artifacts, and larger-scale load testing would further strengthen confidence in these figures ahead of a municipal-scale deployment.

\section{Project Contributions}
\label{sec:project-contributions}

The main contribution is the integration of contract-enforced civic reporting with off-chain processing in one working, tested prototype. The project provides separate, purpose-built workflows for standard and emergency reports, two-stage community validation and post-resolution review, multisignature authority governance, and public opinion polling, and demonstrates through the Chapter~\ref{ch:testing} evidence that these workflows behave correctly both individually and as an integrated whole.

A second contribution is a clear, well-reasoned separation between public records and trusted off-chain services. Report states and authority actions are recorded on-chain, while identity checking, moderation, queue processing, and media storage remain off-chain behind defined interfaces. This separation, justified in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture} and validated by the passing integration tests in Chapter~\ref{ch:testing}, makes the system's trust boundaries explicit and keeps each component independently testable and replaceable.

A further contribution is the use of government-signed single-use tickets and pseudonyms as a practical, fully implemented prototype for controlled, privacy-respecting participation. This mechanism gives citizens a genuine way to report, validate, and vote without exposing their identity on the public ledger. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} also situates this contribution against existing citizen-reporting and blockchain-governance frameworks, showing that the combination of privacy, moderation, community validation, emergency handling, and governance features implemented here is broader than what is reported in the individual systems reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}.

\section{Scope for Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}

The results in this project establish a solid, tested foundation for a blockchain-based, community-assisted, local-governance reporting framework. As with any prototype developed within an academic timeline, several aspects were deliberately scoped to keep the project achievable while still exercising the full reporting workflow. These represent clear, well-understood opportunities for the framework to grow rather than defects in what has been built.

The identity layer is implemented as a simulator that issues signed, single-use tickets rather than a full anonymous-credential or zero-knowledge system. This was a deliberate choice, explained in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}, that let the complete reporting, voting, and governance workflow be designed and demonstrated now behind an interface that a stronger privacy protocol can adopt later without disturbing the rest of the system. The AI moderation layer is a centralized off-chain aggregator rather than a decentralized oracle network. Its test cases are based on a curated set of examples rather than a large, representative, multilingual civic dataset. The relayer, ticket issuer, Redis instance, AI aggregator, and IPFS service are trusted components in the current design, consistent with the layered trust boundaries set out in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}. An independent security audit and broader penetration testing would add further assurance on top of the automated test evidence already gathered. Frontend automated testing, a formal accessibility review, and a supervised real-world pilot were likewise outside the scope of the current testing cycle and are natural next steps once the underlying componentsn are extended toward production use.

\section{Prioritized Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Building on the tested foundation described above, future work is recommended in the following order.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item   {Introduce stronger privacy credentials.} The identity simulator can be extended into a reviewed anonymous-credential or zero-knowledge protocol, allowing eligibility and single-use actions to be proved without exposing a government identifier or a stable citizen key, while reusing the same ticket-and-nullifier interface already validated by the Security suite.

    \item   {Broaden AI moderation evidence.} Collecting a larger, representative Sinhala, Tamil, and English civic dataset, with suitable consent and governance, would extend the current passing test evidence into broader accuracy, fairness, and robustness figures, and support the addition of a human review and appeal path for contested moderation decisions.

    \item   {Evaluate a decentralized oracle configuration.} A decentrally governed alternative to the current centralized AI aggregator can be trialed and compared against it for cost, latency, accountability, and failure handling, informing whether and how to adopt it.

    \item   {Obtain independent security assurance.} An external audit of the smart contracts and relayer, together with formal verification of key invariants such as nullifier uniqueness and access control, would add independent assurance on top of the 75 passing contract cases and 7 passing security cases already recorded.

    \item   {Strengthen operational resilience.} Redis replication and automatic failover, documented IPFS pinning and backup policies, and clear procedures for validator admission, rotation, and recovery would extend the current single-host-per-service deployment toward one suited for continuous operation.

    \item   {Test the frontend and accessibility.} Automated unit, integration, and end-to-end tests for citizen, authority, and governance interface paths, together with a structured accessibility and browser-compatibility review, would extend the current backend and integration test coverage to the interface layer.

    \item   {Run a supervised municipal pilot.} A small pilot deployment within the university or a partnering local authority conducted after the security and resilience work above would validate practical usability, privacy expectations, and reporting workflows in a real institutional setting, using measures such as a System Usability Scale study, task completion rates, and operational load observations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Final Conclusion}
\label{sec:final-conclusion}

This project has produced a working, thoroughly tested prototype for blockchain-supported local-governance reporting. The private PoA chain, four smart contracts, relayer pipeline, IPFS storage, web interfaces, identity simulator, and AI moderation service are integrated into a single coherent system, and 130 passing automated test cases, a dedicated performance benchmark, and a complete end-to-end lifecycle scenario together confirm that this integration works as designed.

The strongest project goals, a permissioned blockchain, contract-enforced workflows, and full component integration, have been clearly achieved and are backed by comprehensive test evidence. Citizen and authority interfaces, AI-based content moderation, and privacy-preserving pseudonymous participation have also been achieved at the level appropriate for a prototype, with well-identified opportunities to extend each of them further, stronger privacy credentials, broader AI evaluation, decentralized moderation governance, independent security assurance, and real-world piloting, as summarized above. The project therefore demonstrates clear technical feasibility for a privacy-conscious, community-assisted, blockchain-based approach to local-governance reporting. It leaves behind a tested, extensible foundation from which these next steps can be pursued with confidence.
